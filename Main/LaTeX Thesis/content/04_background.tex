\chapter{Background: The Events of 2022--2026}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter establishes the institutional setting of the episodes the empirical chapters analyse and fixes the event dates that define the windows used in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. For each episode it documents what happened, how official institutions characterised the market backdrop, and how the shock is classified under the originator--nature taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:fw_taxonomy}; each episode section closes with the response the safe-haven hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} predicts \emph{ex ante}, so that Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reads as a test of stated predictions rather than a search for patterns. The scholarly debate these episodes feed into is reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} and is not repeated here.

\section{Liberation Day and the U.S.\ Tariff Shock}
\label{sec:bg_tariff}

On 2~April 2025, in an announcement branded ``Liberation Day,'' the United States imposed
broad ``reciprocal'' tariffs on nearly all of its trading partners, combining a flat
baseline rate with higher country-specific surcharges \parencite{kamin_2025}.
The measure represented an abrupt change in the U.S.\ trade-policy regime rather than a
marginal adjustment, and official assessments subsequently documented the resulting trade
fragmentation and the pass-through of higher tariffs to economic activity
\parencite{ecb_2025_fsr,ecb_2026_bulletin}.

The market reaction was one of broad risk aversion: equities sold off, implied volatility
rose, and crude oil fell as market participants priced weaker global demand \parencite{ecb_2025_fsr}.
Around the announcements, the dollar depreciated against the euro, yen and Swiss franc
even as risk metrics deteriorated, and long-dated Treasury yields rose while the currency
weakened \parencite{kamin_2025}. The competing interpretations of this break---a repricing
of the dollar's reserve status and an erosion of its safety premium---are reviewed in
Section~\ref{sec:lit_tariffs}.

Under the taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:fw_taxonomy}, Liberation Day is the study's clearest
U.S.-authored economic-policy shock, and the accompanying fall in crude marks it as a
demand-side disturbance. A partial ninety-day tariff
pause on 9~April 2025 tempered the equity drawdown without restoring the dollar
\parencite{kamin_2025}, and is treated here as a separately dated reversal. Under the
safe-haven hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}, a U.S.-authored shock that erodes the
dollar's safety premium should weaken the currency on announcement and recover, at most,
only partially when the policy is partially walked back.

\section{The Israel--Iran Escalation and the Strait of Hormuz}
\label{sec:bg_iran}

The Gulf episode unfolded in stages, from threat to realisation. A first, twelve-day confrontation in
June~2025 -- Israeli and U.S.\ strikes on Iranian facilities, an Iranian response, and a
rapid ceasefire -- lifted crude only briefly and left major currencies little changed
\parencite{kilian_plante_richter_zhou_2026}. Escalation resumed in early 2026 and turned acute on 28~February 2026, when the United States
and Israel launched airstrikes on Iran. Iran retaliated and moved to close the Strait of
Hormuz -- the maritime chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of globally traded oil
passes -- declaring it closed on 2~March and claiming complete control on 4~March 2026
\parencite{fattouh_economou_2026}. Attacks on
shipping followed and Brent crude rose sharply, trading above \$100 per barrel at the height
of the disruption \parencite{imf_2026_menap}, with \textcite{kilian_plante_richter_zhou_2026} tracing
the inflationary consequences through the oil channel.

Under the taxonomy of Section~\ref{sec:fw_taxonomy}, the crisis is a military/oil-supply
shock in which the United States is involved as a co-belligerent, and the rise in crude
identifies it as a supply-driven disturbance -- the mirror image of the demand-side tariff
shock. Several further dated events punctuate the episode. A two-week ceasefire announced on
7~April 2026 committed to reopening the Strait, but collapsed within days when the Islamabad
talks failed and the United States imposed a naval blockade on 13~April \parencite{imf_2026_menap}.
A round of U.S.\ strikes followed on 25~May 2026, and the war was wound down only by a
separate sixty-day ceasefire announced on 11~June 2026 (signed 17~June), which reopened the
Strait. The April truce furnishes the within-crisis reversal the analysis exploits: under the
safe-haven hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}, a military/oil-supply shock should draw
a haven bid into the dollar as the threat builds and release it on credible de-escalation, so
that any premium the closure put into the dollar should come back out as the Strait is set to
reopen. The June ceasefire falls too close to the end of the sample for a full event window
(Section~\ref{sec:es_robust}).

\section{The Ukraine Control}
\label{sec:bg_ukraine}

The Russian invasion of Ukraine on 24~February 2022 serves as the external benchmark: a
military/oil-supply shock the United States did not author and in which it was not a
belligerent. The invasion combined a sharp spike in geopolitical risk with a supply-driven jump in
energy prices, both visible in Figures~\ref{fig:ov_oil} and~\ref{fig:ov_gpr}. Episodes of
this kind are the empirical reference point for the classical safe-haven response reviewed
in Section~\ref{sec:lit_safe_haven}: in risk-off events the dollar, yen and Swiss franc tend
to appreciate against other currencies.

Because the United States is here a bystander rather than a protagonist, the invasion
isolates the \emph{nature} of the shock from U.S.\ authorship. Under the hypothesis of
Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} the dollar should display the classical haven response, making
the 2022 invasion the benchmark against which the U.S.-authored tariff shock and the
U.S.-involved Hormuz crisis are compared.

\section{The Structure of the Comparison}
\label{sec:bg_design}

Taken together, these three episodes populate the originator--nature taxonomy of
Section~\ref{sec:fw_taxonomy} in a way that gives the comparison its identifying power. The
invasion of Ukraine on 24~February 2022 is the external benchmark, a military/oil-supply
shock the United States did not author; the Liberation Day tariffs of 2~April 2025 are the
U.S.-authored trade-policy shock; and the Hormuz crisis, which began with the U.S.--Israeli
airstrikes of 28~February 2026 and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz on 2~March, is a
military/oil-supply shock in which the United States is implicated as a co-belligerent.
Holding the crisis character of the shock roughly fixed while varying its origin is what
allows the dollar's response to be attributed to the \emph{nature} of a shock rather than to
U.S.\ authorship, or the reverse: if origin governs the response, the two U.S.-linked
episodes should move the dollar alike and apart from Ukraine, whereas if nature governs it,
the two military/oil-supply shocks should resemble each other regardless of who authored
them.

Around each treatment sits a dated reversal that sharpens the reading. The ninety-day tariff
pause of 9~April 2025 partially unwinds the Liberation Day shock, and the two-week ceasefire
of 7~April 2026 unwinds the Hormuz threat; if a safe-haven premium moves the dollar on the
shock, it should move back, at least in part, on its reversal. Two additional
military/oil-supply episodes -- the twelve-day Israel--Iran war that began on 13~June 2025 and
the U.S.\ strikes of 25~May 2026 -- provide further events against which the main results can
be checked. Each of these dates is treated as the centre, $t_0$, of the event window defined
in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, and announcement dates are used throughout, since these are
the points at which the information reaches markets.
